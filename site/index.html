About this tracker

This tracks every newly built or permitted multiplex in Toronto since 2022, refreshed daily from the City's Open Data. Each permit creates between one and six homes — a duplex, triplex, fourplex, five- or six-plex, a laneway or garden suite, or a secondary suite in an existing house.

Map shows where they are, one dot per permit, with wards shaded by how many units their permits add up to. Trends shows the same permits over time, by month, quarter or year, counted either as applications or as the homes they create. Both views read the same filters, so switching between them keeps whatever you have narrowed down — and the address bar carries that slice, so a link shares the exact view you are looking at.

What's included

Toronto legalized fourplexes citywide in 2023 and extended as-of-right permissions up to six units in parts of the city in 2024, so the map caps at six units per permit to match. A permit is included when the City records it as creating at least one dwelling unit and its structure type is not a single-family detached house.

Why some permits aren't new buildings

A permit's structure type only records how many units result, not how much got built — "2 Unit – Detached" covers a ground-up duplex, a basement apartment legalized inside an existing house, and a garden suite behind one. Most multiplex-scale permits (2022–2026) are not new buildings. So each permit is also classified by where its new units come from, read from the permit's own description:

  • New building — a whole new building, including demolishing something first and rebuilding.
  • Laneway / garden suite — a separate small dwelling in the back yard, often built over or inside an existing garage.
  • New basement unit(s) — a unit added in the basement of a building that was already standing. Hidden by default: it's the most common permit here and the least visible change, and it's the same act whether the house above it was a single-family home or already a triplex.
  • New aboveground unit(s) — a unit added above grade in a building that was already standing, by interior conversion or an addition.

Turn any of them on or off with the Construction type filter above. Permits whose description describes no new unit at all — a deck, a porch, a garage rebuild — are left off the map entirely, even where the City's own unit count says otherwise.

Where the data comes from

Everything is pulled straight from Toronto Open Data — the active and cleared building permit datasets, geocoded against the City's municipal address points and joined to ward boundaries. A scheduled job refreshes the whole chain daily. You can download the exact data behind the map as GeoJSON, and the code that builds it is on GitHub.

Caveats worth knowing

A permit is not a finished building — active permits may stall or be abandoned, and cleared permits are the only ones confirmed complete. Permits whose address can't be matched to an address point are counted in the ward totals but can't be drawn as points.

Unit counts are gross, not net. The City publishes a "dwelling units lost" figure but leaves it at zero on virtually every permit, so there is no reliable way to subtract what a demolition removed. A permit that tears down a house and builds a fourplex counts as four units here, not three.
